Activity 14A: Tokenisation across languages

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how the language balance of a training corpus affects the tokens a tokenizer learns.
  • Compare English word boundaries and both languages’ token counts across different training balances.
  • Distinguish frequent character sequences from meaningful language units.

Background

A tokenizer splits text into tokens: whole words or smaller pieces. Here, byte pair encoding (BPE) learns these pieces by repeatedly joining the most frequent neighbouring pair of letters or pieces in training text.

What happens to English words when a tokenizer learns mostly from another language? Here, that language is Mandarin written in pinyin (Roman letters, without tone marks). You will investigate familiar English words; English meanings accompany the Mandarin examples. You do not need to read, pronounce or translate Mandarin. This is a small teaching experiment, not a commercial language model.

Instructions

  1. Start with 90% pinyin and 10% English training sentences. Predict: will familiar English words stay whole or split into many pieces?
  2. Select Train tokenizer. Read the English test sentence and inspect its pieces. Each outlined block is one token.
  3. Change the training balance to 50/50, then 10/90, and train again. Keep the same test sentence while comparing your results. The actual training sentences are shown in step 1.
  4. Compare one English word across your runs in step 2. Then follow each language’s token counts down the table. Try another sentence, then explain your observations in step 3.

        About this simulation: all input is lowercase, uses Roman letters, and has no punctuation or tone marks. The character-level implementation has no byte fallback or special word-end symbol. The three corpora take 9/1, 5/5 or 1/9 sentences from fixed language-specific lists. Each displayed sentence is counted once, with no hidden weighting. Corpus size is fixed at 10 sentences, but word counts, character counts and vocabulary also change. These examples illustrate an effect of training data; they do not isolate language balance from every other factor. Pairs are recounted after each merge; tied counts use a fixed ordering, so repeating a run gives the same rules. Test sentences never update the rules. Real tokenizers may use bytes, different boundaries, much larger corpora and larger vocabularies.

        Reflection & Discussion Guide

        1. Compare the same English sentence under 90/10 and 10/90 training. Identify one word whose token boundaries changed. Explain why.
        2. Find a piece of an English word, such as a piece of studying or library. Does that piece have a meaning on its own? What does BPE count when it learns pieces?
        3. Keep the same test sentence selected. Compare the English token counts across 90/10, 50/50 and 10/90, then do the same for pinyin. Does one balance give both languages their lowest count? Support your answer with numbers from the table.
        4. Imagine English were poorly represented in a real tokenizer’s training data. If a system charges per token or limits token counts, how might splitting familiar English words into more pieces affect you? What would you need to test before generalising from this small experiment?

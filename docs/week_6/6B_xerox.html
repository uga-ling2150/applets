Activity: The Uncooperative Copier

Learning Objectives

  • Experience firsthand the gap between having a clear goal and knowing how to make an unfamiliar system do it
  • Connect your own moment-to-moment improvising to Lucy Suchman's distinction between plans (what you intended to do) and situated actions (what you actually did, worked out step by step as the interface responded to you)

Background

In the early 1980s, researchers at Xerox PARC — including anthropologist Lucy Suchman — set up video cameras next to a brand-new photocopier and simply watched people try to use it. Customers had been complaining that the machine was too complicated, even though Xerox's own advertising claimed "all you have to do is push the green button."

The footage told a different story. The green button really was easy to find. The trouble started as soon as someone needed the machine to do anything more specific — collate, staple, print on both sides — and had to guess their way through menus that didn't explain themselves. Suchman's conclusion wasn't that the button should have been a different color. It was that people don't operate machines by executing a plan from start to finish; they feel their way through, moment by moment, revising their idea of what's going on as the machine talks back to them (or doesn't).

Below is a small, deliberately unhelpful copier simulator built to let you feel a version of that same confusion. It is not a realistic model of how modern copiers work under the hood — it's a simplified stand-in, built to be opaque on purpose rather than to be technically accurate.

One more thing: pay attention to what you're copying from, not just what settings you're copying to. The job ticket describes both.

Instructions

You'll be given a job ticket with a task on it. Your job is to get the machine to complete that task — nothing more. The interface will not explain itself to you, and that's the point. Wrong turns, dead ends, and moments of "wait, what did that just do?" are exactly the data this activity is trying to produce.

When you finish (or decide you've had enough), a debrief will unlock showing everything you clicked, in order. Bring that log to your group discussion.

Finishing: only "Staple + Collate" produces bound, ordered sets. Stack and Collate on their own won't satisfy a job that needs both.
Sides: found under Settings. Pick the option that turns single-sided originals into double-sided output.
Aux Access needs a facility login and isn't part of a normal copy job.
Interrupt only matters if a job is already running — it does nothing when the queue is empty.
Tray 2 feeds the job. An empty or jammed tray blocks Start no matter how complete the spec is — the release is built into the housing below the control panel, not on it.

💬 Discussion Question (Group Activity)

Look at your own log line by line. At the moment you pressed each button, did you have a plan for what would happen — or were you reacting to whatever the screen had just shown you? Where in the log does your account of "what I was doing" stop matching what you were actually clicking?

💬 Discussion Question (Group Activity)

If you used the field service reference, what made it useful (or not)? Real manuals for expert users often work the same way — technically correct, but written for someone who already knows most of the answer.

Reflection & Discussion Guide

Once your group has compared logs, discuss:
  • The "big green button" story is often told as if Suchman's study was about making the start button easier to find. Based on what you just experienced, what was the study actually about?
  • If you tried the task more than once, did having a rough mental model from your first attempt change how you behaved the second time — even though the machine hadn't changed at all?
  • Suchman's original footage wasn't only about menus — people also fumbled with covers, trays, and levers whose purpose wasn't obvious just from looking at them. When did you first realize a physical part of this machine (not a button on the panel) needed opening? What made that moment feel different from working through a menu?
  • If your second attempt's original wasn't bound, did you remember to check the document glass anyway — or did you carry your first attempt's solution forward without re-checking whether it still applied? What does that say about the difference between "a plan that worked once" and "a machine that remembers what you last did to it"?
  • Chatbots and voice assistants don't have a "screen with menus" in the way a copier does. Where does the equivalent confusion show up when the system you're talking to is a language model instead of a physical machine?

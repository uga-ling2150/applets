Activity 7C: Sequence Expansion

Learning Objectives

  • Identify adjacency pairs and their first and second pair parts.
  • Distinguish pre-expansions, insert expansions and post-expansions by where they occur in relation to a base pair.
  • Represent how sequences are built from other sequences and depend on them.

Background

An adjacency pair is two turns by different speakers. The first pair part (FPP), such as a question, request or invitation, makes a particular kind of second pair part (SPP) relevant next: an answer, a granting or refusal, an acceptance or declination. Conversation is rarely a string of separate pairs. Instead, a base pair can be expanded by other sequences in three positions:

  • Pre-expansion, before the base FPP. “Are you doing anything on Saturday?” checks whether an invitation is worth making.
  • Insert expansion, between the base FPP and SPP. A post-first insert deals with the FPP itself, often a repair (“Sorry, a large what?”). A pre-second insert sets up what the SPP needs (“For here or to go?”).
  • Post-expansion, after the base SPP. It is minimal when it is a single turn that closes the sequence, a sequence-closing third (SCT) such as “Oh”, “Okay” or “Great”. It is non-minimal when it opens a further pair (“Really?” – “Yeah”).

Expansions are sequences too, so they can be expanded in turn. In this applet you draw the structure: a bracket joins two turns into a pair, and a dependency arrow says which base turn an expansion belongs to. The applet names the expansion from where it sits.

Instructions

  1. Choose a transcript, or paste your own. Read it through before you mark anything.
  2. Pair turns: drag from one turn’s dot to another turn’s dot. The earlier turn is tagged FPP and the later one SPP. You can also click one dot, then the other.
  3. Attach expansions: press on a turn and release it on the turn it depends on. Use the base FPP for pre-expansions and post-first inserts, and the base SPP for pre-second inserts and post-expansions. Dragging a turn that is already in a pair moves the whole pair. To attach a longer sequence, start from its bracket.
  4. Join sequences: drag from one bracket’s dot to another bracket’s dot.
  5. Click any turn, bracket dot or arrow to check or change it in the panel below the transcript. Press Delete or Backspace to remove its annotation: the dependency first, then the bracket. Then compare your analysis with a partner’s and with the suggested one.

      Tags and colours
      • FPP / SPP: first and second pair part of an adjacency pair.
      • base (red): a pair that other sequences expand.
      • pre (teal): pre-expansion, placed before and attached to the base FPP.
      • ins (blue): insert expansion, between the base FPP and SPP. It is attached to the FPP (post-first) or the SPP (pre-second).
      • post (brown): non-minimal post-expansion, a pair after and attached to the base SPP.
      • SCT (brown): sequence-closing third, a single-turn (minimal) post-expansion.
      • A dashed bracket joins two pairs or sequences into a larger sequence. An arrow on the right points from an expansion to the turn it depends on.

      Reflection & Discussion Guide

      1. In “At the counter”, why is “Sorry, a large what?” a post-first insert, but “For here or to go?” a pre-second insert? What does each one need from the other speaker?
      2. In “Saturday plans”, the pre-expansion contains its own insert expansion. What does this tell you about how far sequences can be built inside one another?
      3. Where did you and a partner (or the suggested analysis) disagree about which turn an expansion depends on? What in the talk supports each view?
      4. What would each conversation lose without its sequence-closing third (“Oh wow.”, “Great.”)? Is a minimal post-expansion ever required?
      5. When a chatbot asks a clarifying question before answering, or when a user types “thanks” after an answer, which kinds of expansion are these? How might a system designer use this?

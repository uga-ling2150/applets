Watch a Decoder-Only Transformer Finish a Sentence

Like the RNN version of this demo, a decoder-only transformer writes text by predicting one word at a time, then feeding its own guess back in as if it were just another word someone typed. The generation loop is identical.

What's different is how it decides what comes next. At each step it looks directly back at every earlier word and works out how much attention to pay to each one — that's self-attention. Real transformers do this several times in parallel, with each attention head often specializing in a different kind of relationship. This bar chart is a stand-in for the vector the model holds for the current word once all the attention heads' findings have been combined — sometimes still called a hidden state, even in a transformer, since every position gets one at every layer. It isn't passed along one step at a time like an RNN's is; it's rebuilt at each step from whatever the attention heads just gathered. What the model thinks comes next

Curious how the "prediction," "attention," and "representation" numbers were made?

Just like the RNN version, this demo uses a small pre-set script instead of a real trained model, so the focus stays on the big idea: feed a word in, get a guess out, feed the guess back in. A real transformer calculates next-word probabilities, every attention head's weights, and every representation vector from millions of learned parameters — and typically uses far more than three heads, stacked across many layers.

Several things this demo does keep faithful to how real decoder-only transformers work:

  • While reading a sentence you already gave it, the model still predicts the next word at every step — we just don't use that guess, because we already know the true next word. Using the true word instead of the model's guess is called teacher forcing, same as before.
  • The causal mask in the heatmap is real: a decoder is built so a word can only attend to itself and words before it, never one that comes later.
  • Different heads really do tend to specialize. "Head 3" here always leans on the very first word — real trained models frequently show this exact pattern, sometimes nicknamed an attention sink.

One more thing worth noticing as you go: an RNN has to carry everything relevant in one running summary that updates step by step, so details from far earlier can fade. A transformer's attention lets a word reach all the way back and look directly at any earlier word, no matter the distance.

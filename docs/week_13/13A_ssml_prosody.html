Activity 13A: Prosody with SSML

Learning Objectives

  • Describe what the common SSML tags do to a synthetic voice: pauses, emphasis, pitch, rate, volume and pronunciation.
  • Use SSML to move the prosodic focus of an utterance, and explain how the meaning changes.
  • Predict where a listener will hear a transition-relevance place (TRP) in synthetic speech, and move one by editing the script.

Background

A voice assistant doesn't only choose what to say. Its text-to-speech (TTS) engine also has to decide how to say it: which word carries the main accent, where to pause, and where the pitch falls to signal that a turn is over. Engines make these choices from punctuation and grammar, and they often get them wrong. Speech Synthesis Markup Language (SSML) is the standard way for a designer to override them. Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant and Microsoft's voices all accept it.

Two of the prosodic features from earlier weeks matter most here. Prosodic focus is the word that gets the strongest accent; it marks new or contrastive information (“I said the red one”). A transition-relevance place (TRP) is a point where a turn could be complete, so another speaker may come in. In speech, TRPs are signalled by a complete grammatical unit, a falling or rising final pitch, and a pause. A voice assistant that pauses in the wrong place invites the user to talk over it.

Instructions

  1. Meet the tags. Read the table of common SSML tags and listen to each example.
  2. Listen to the sample conversation. Play the call between a bus-line assistant and a caller, and match what you hear to the tags in the script.
  3. Edit the script. Move the prosodic focus in one line, then move a TRP in an assistant turn. Save each version so you can compare them, and download your work before you leave.

1

Meet the tags

SSML looks like HTML: words are wrapped in tags that say how they should sound. Every script starts with <speak> and ends with </speak>. Most tags come in pairs and wrap the words they affect, such as <emphasis>red</emphasis>. A few, like <break/>, stand alone and end with />.

Click Listen to hear each example. Compare it with how the same words sound with no tags at all.

Common SSML tags
TagWhat it doesExample
<speak> Wraps the whole script. Everything to be spoken goes inside. <speak>Your bus is on its way.</speak>
<voice name="…"> Changes the speaker. In this applet, each <voice> is one turn, and you choose which browser voice plays each name. <voice name="Assistant">Where to?</voice> <voice name="Caller">The library.</voice>
<p>, <s> Mark a paragraph and a sentence. The voice gives each sentence its own final intonation; a paragraph adds a longer pause. <p><s>Route ten is late</s><s>take route eleven</s></p>
<break/> Inserts a silent pause, either a length (time="500ms", time="1s") or a strength from x-weak to x-strong. Your bus leaves at ten. <break time="1s"/> From Tate.
<emphasis> Stresses the words inside: level="strong", "moderate" (the default) or "reduced" (de-stressed). This is how you place prosodic focus. I said the <emphasis level="strong">red</emphasis> one.
<prosody> Sets pitch, rate and volume directly. Values are words (x-low to x-high, x-slow to x-fast, x-soft to x-loud) or changes such as "+20%" or "-2st" (semitones). That's <prosody pitch="x-high" rate="slow">amazing</prosody>.
<say-as> Says how to read numbers and abbreviations: interpret-as="characters" (spell it out), "telephone", "time", "date" (with format="mdy") or "ordinal". Call <say-as interpret-as="telephone">706-555-0142</say-as> about <say-as interpret-as="characters">UGA</say-as>.
<sub alias="…"> Says the alias instead of the written words. Meet me at <sub alias="the Tate Student Center">Tate</sub>.
<phoneme> Gives an exact pronunciation in IPA (alphabet="ipa" ph="…"). Browser voices can't do this, so this applet reads the spelling. I say <phoneme alphabet="ipa" ph="təˈmɑːtoʊ">tomato</phoneme>.
<mark name="…"/> A silent bookmark. Apps use it to time events to the speech. Here, use <mark name="TRP"/> to label where you think a TRP is; it shows as a flag in the prosody view. That's all. <mark name="TRP"/>
About this player. Your browser's voices don't read SSML themselves. This applet splits the script into chunks and plays each chunk with its own pitch, rate and volume, so a stressed word is spoken as a separate piece and you may hear a small gap around it. Engines that read SSML natively, such as Amazon Polly or Azure Speech, join the pieces more smoothly. The voices also sound different from browser to browser.

Listen to the sample conversation

The script below is a short call to a campus bus line. Click Play the conversation, and follow along in the prosody view underneath the script. The view draws each chunk the way it will be spoken: higher on the line for higher pitch, larger for louder or stressed, and spread out for slower. Gaps are pauses.

The view also flags each possible TRP: every place where a sentence ends with ., ? or !, so the voice gives it final intonation. The pause after it decides how strongly it invites the other speaker in. A red flag in the middle of a turn is a place where a real caller would be likely to start talking.

Edit freely. The prosody view updates as you type. If a tag is broken, a message below says which line to fix.

    Move the focus and the TRPs

    Now edit the script in Step 2. Change one thing at a time, play it, and click Save this version with a short label so you can come back and compare.

    Task A: Move the prosodic focus

    1. In the assistant's third turn, Right. Eleven, not ten., the focus is on Eleven. Move the <emphasis level="strong"> tag onto not, and then onto ten. What would the caller have had to say for each version to be the natural reply?
    2. In Take route eleven from the Tate Center, the focus is on eleven. Move it to Tate. Now what does the assistant seem to be correcting?
    3. Find a word that is given information (already said by the other speaker), stress it with level="strong", and listen. Then try level="reduced" on it. Which sounds more natural, and why?

    Task B: Move the TRPs

    1. The assistant's second turn has a TRP in the middle, after Take route eleven from the Tate Center., with a long break. Remove it: shorten or delete the <break> and change the full stop to a comma, so the turn holds the floor until the end.
    2. Now do the opposite. In the last assistant turn, the phone number is buried at the end of one long sentence. Add a TRP before it: end the sentence after the date, add a <break time="800ms"/>, and continue with a new sentence for the number. Does the caller now get a chance to say “yes, go ahead” or “no thanks”?
    3. Make a question with no TRP after it: put more speech right after Where are you heading today? with no pause. Does the question still sound like it wants an answer?
    4. Label each TRP you intend with <mark name="TRP"/>. Do your labels line up with the TRP flags the prosody view finds?

      Reflection & Discussion Guide

      1. Choose one line where you moved the focus. Quote both versions and explain how the meaning, or the conversation it fits into, changed.
      2. Which cues made a TRP sound strongest to you: the end of a sentence, the final pitch, or the length of the pause? Use one of your versions as evidence.
      3. Voice assistants often pause mid-turn and then talk over a user who responds. Using what you found, how would you write the SSML for a long answer (such as directions) so the user knows when they are expected to speak?
      4. SSML lets a designer choose stress and pauses in advance. What can't it do that a human speaker does during a conversation?

Activity 11A: Style and Persona

Learning Objectives

  • Identify the language cues (word choice, hedging, pronouns, punctuation) that make a speaker sound like a particular kind of person.
  • Compare how two system prompts change the style of a model's answer to the same question.
  • Support an interpretation of persona with exact phrases, and test whether it holds across generations.

Background

A persona is the identity a speaker presents through language: a careful tutor, an enthusiastic friend, a brisk customer-service agent. We recognise personas from small choices, such as “you could” versus “you should”, “let’s” versus “you”, or an exclamation mark at the end of a sentence.

Chat models are given a persona through a system prompt: an instruction, usually hidden from the user, that tells the model who to be and how to talk. In this activity you will give two personas the same question and examine how the system prompt shows up in the wording of their answers. Use exact phrases to support your interpretation.

Instructions

  1. Generate two answers. Keep the default question and system prompts for your first try, and click Generate both answers.
  2. Annotate the language. Select a phrase in either answer, say what kind of feature it is, and explain what it suggests about the persona. Collect evidence from both answers.
  3. Change one thing and compare again. Edit one system prompt, generate again, and see whether your evidence still holds. Download your work before you leave.

Language makes a persona

Change the prompt. Compare the language. Explain the evidence.

1

Give two personas the same question

Before a chat model writes anything, it reads two kinds of instruction. The user prompt is the question you type. The system prompt is set up in advance by whoever deploys the model, and tells it who it is supposed to be and how it should talk. Users rarely see the system prompt, but it shapes every reply.

Here you write both system prompts yourself. Persona A and Persona B get exactly the same question, from the same model, with the same sampling settings. The only thing that differs is the system prompt, so if the two answers sound different, the system prompt is the most likely reason. (Step 3 shows that some of the difference is also down to chance.)

For your first try, keep the defaults and click Generate both answers. The two defaults are deliberately close: both personas are asked to be helpful and accurate, but one is a careful tutor and the other an enthusiastic study partner. Notice that neither prompt lists any words to use. The model has to turn a description of a social role into word choices by itself.

Persona is built from language. You can't see the tutor's office or hear the study partner's tone of voice. Everything that tells you who is speaking, such as how formal the words are, how sure the speaker sounds and whether they say “you” or “we”, is in the text. That text is the evidence you'll collect in Step 2.
Technical glossary
  • System prompt: An instruction given to a chat model before the conversation starts. It sets the model's role, tone and rules. In deployed chatbots it is written by the company, not the user.
  • User prompt: The message the user types. Here it is the same question for both personas.
  • Persona: The identity a speaker presents through their way of speaking. A persona is a performance, not a fact about the speaker: the same model can perform many.
  • Style: The pattern of linguistic choices (vocabulary, sentence length, punctuation, how direct or hedged a claim is) that we use to recognise a persona.

Your question and system prompts are sent to the model service; your annotations stay on this page. Practice answers were written by the course team for when the service can't be reached. They don't change when you edit the prompts.

2

Annotate the language

🔒 Locked. This step opens when you generate two answers (or use the practice answers) in Step 1.

Read the two answers side by side, the way a linguist would. The question isn't which answer is better, but how each one makes the speaker sound like a particular kind of person. Look for small, specific choices: a modal verb such as could or should, an exclamation mark, a pronoun, a casual word where a formal one was possible.

To annotate, drag across a few words in either answer. A small window opens with your phrase filled in. Choose the kind of feature it shows, then write a sentence or two linking the wording to the persona: what does this choice suggest about the speaker's role, attitude, or relationship with you? Your annotations are listed under each answer and highlighted in yellow in the text. On a touchscreen or keyboard, use the Annotate button under an answer and type the exact words.

Five kinds of feature to look for
  • Address and relationship: how the speaker refers to you or includes you (“you”, “we”, “let’s”). Does it sound like an expert advising you, or a friend alongside you?
  • Certainty and hedging: how strongly a claim or suggestion is made (cautious “could”, “might”, “it may help” versus direct “will” or a bare command).
  • Register and vocabulary: formal, technical or conversational word choices (“retrieval practice” versus “quiz yourself”).
  • Evaluation and affect: words and punctuation that show judgement or feeling (“great”, “tricky”, an exclamation mark).
  • Politeness and directness: how an instruction is softened or made direct (“you might consider” versus “do this”).
Worked example: from phrase to annotation

Phrase: “You could” in the Persona A practice answer.
Feature: Certainty and hedging.
Explanation: “Could” offers a suggestion instead of a command. This cautious wording leaves the decision to the learner, which fits a careful tutor who doesn't want to overstate what will work.

This is a teaching example, not a claim about your own generated answer. Use phrases that actually appear in your answers. If you see no clear difference between the personas, that is a finding too: explain it with language evidence.

          3

          Change one thing and compare again

          🔒 Locked. This step opens when you have two answers to compare in Step 2.

          One pair of answers is a single observation. To find out what the system prompt is really doing, change one thing in Step 1 and generate again. Keep the question the same. For example, remove “use measured language” from Persona A, make Persona B “a strict coach”, or swap the two prompts. Then look for your Step 2 features in the new answers: did the hedges disappear? Did “let’s” move to the other persona?

          Also try generating again without changing anything. The model picks each word by sampling from a probability distribution (Activity 11B shows how), so the same prompts can give different wording on each run. A feature that appears every time for one persona is probably coming from the system prompt. A feature that comes and goes may just be chance.

          When a new pair of answers loads, the previous pair and its annotations move to Saved comparisons below. Annotate the new pair too, so you can compare evidence across runs.

          A fair test changes one thing. If you edit both system prompts and the question at the same time, you can't tell which edit caused which change in the wording. Change one variable, keep the rest fixed, and repeat the run before drawing a conclusion.

          “Keep this comparison” saves a snapshot of the current answers and annotations below without generating again; click it again after more annotating to update the snapshot. “Download my work” saves every comparison, prompt, answer and annotation as one file. Nothing is submitted, and comparisons are lost if you close or reload the page.

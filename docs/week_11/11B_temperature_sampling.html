Activity 11B: Temperature Sampling

Learning Objectives

  • Explain how a language model turns counts from its training data into next-word probabilities, and how it chooses a word from them.
  • Distinguish settings that reshape the probabilities (temperature) from settings that remove candidates (top-k and top-p).
  • Compare repeated outputs from a real language model, using quoted language as evidence of how predictable or distinctive the wording is.

Background

Language models write one piece at a time. At each step they give every possible next word a probability, choose one, and repeat. How they make that choice is controlled by sampling settings, of which temperature is the best known. The same model with the same prompt can sound careful and repetitive, or varied and surprising, depending on these settings.

You'll start with a tiny model that you can see inside completely, and finish by testing the same settings on a real language model.

Instructions

  1. Generate a sentence. Keep the default training sentences and click Generate a whole sentence. Try it a few times.
  2. Inspect a word choice. Pick one step of your sentence, change one setting at a time, and compare the probability charts.
  3. Compare real outputs. This step unlocks after you finish a sentence in step 1. Keep the prompt fixed, generate at least twice per setting, and quote examples of how the wording changes.

1

Generate a sentence with a tiny model

Every language model, from this tiny one to the model behind a chatbot, does the same basic job: given the text so far, predict what comes next. It doesn't commit to one answer. It gives every possible next word a probability, picks one, adds it to the text, and repeats.

This is a bigram model, the simplest kind: it only looks at the one previous word, called the context. It learns by counting. In the eight training sentences, “study” is followed by “language” 3 times out of 8, so after “study” the model gives “language” a 3 ÷ 8 = 37.5% probability, and “speech”, “syntax”, “meaning”, “conversation” and “together” 12.5% each. Every sentence starts from an invisible <start> marker and ends when the model picks the <end> marker.

Click Generate a whole sentence. The model begins at <start>, draws a word, makes that word the new context, draws again, and stops when it draws <end>. Generate a few times. The model never changes, but the sentences do, because each step is a weighted random draw: like rolling a die whose sides are sized by probability.

Probability isn't destiny. “Language” is the single most likely word after “study”, but at 37.5% it will be chosen less than half the time. The other 62.5% of the time, a less likely word wins. That is why the same model gives different sentences.
View or edit the training sentences (optional)

These are all the model knows. It counts which word follows which; repeating a sentence counts its word pairs again and makes them more likely. If you edit them, click Build bigram model before generating.

Technical glossary
  • Language model: A system that assigns probabilities to possible next words (or tokens) given the text so far.
  • Bigram: A pair of adjacent words, such as “study language”. A bigram model predicts each word from the previous word only.
  • Context: The text a model uses to make its prediction. Here, just the previous word; in a real chatbot, the whole conversation so far.
  • Probability distribution: The full list of candidate next words with their probabilities, which add up to 100%.
  • Sampling: Choosing the next word at random, in proportion to its probability.
  • <start> and <end>: Markers for the beginning and end of a sentence, so the model can learn which words begin sentences and when to stop.

2

Inspect a word choice and reshape it

🔒 Locked. This step opens when you generate a sentence in Step 1.

Each word in your sentence was one draw from a probability distribution. Choose a step to see the distribution it was drawn from. The left chart shows the base probabilities, straight from the training counts. The right chart shows the final probabilities after the sampling settings are applied. With the defaults (temperature 1, top-k 0, top-p 1) the two charts are identical.

Now change one setting at a time and watch the right chart. Your saved sentence doesn't change; the charts show what the model would draw from at this step under the new settings. Generate again in Step 1 to actually sample with them.

Two kinds of setting
  • Temperature reshapes the distribution without removing anything. Below 1, likely words become likelier and unlikely ones rarer: at 0.5, “language” after “study” rises from 37.5% to about 64%. At 0 the model always takes the top word (greedy decoding). Above 1 the distribution flattens: at 2, “language” falls to about 26%, closer to the others.
  • Top-k removes candidates by rank. It keeps only the k most likely words and rescales them to add up to 100%. Top-k 1 is the same as greedy decoding; 0 keeps every word.
  • Top-p removes candidates by probability mass. It keeps the most likely words until together they reach p, and drops the long tail of unlikely words. At top-p 0.5 after “study”, it keeps “language” (37.5%) and just enough of the next words to reach 50%. Top-q is another name for the same rule, used in some research papers.

The settings are applied in order: temperature, then top-k, then top-p, then the remaining probabilities are rescaled to 100%.

Context (the previous word): <start>. In “we study …”, the context for the third word is “study”.

Temperature 0–2 (0 = always the top word). Top-k 0–50 (0 = keep all). Top-p above 0 up to 1 (1 = keep all).

How the probabilities are calculated

Base probability is the bigram count divided by the total count for this context. For temperature T > 0, each probability p is raised to the power 1/T and the results are rescaled to sum to one. Top-k keeps the k highest probabilities; top-p then keeps the smallest set of top words whose cumulative probability reaches p, after rescaling the top-k candidates. Finally, the remaining weights are rescaled again. Temperature zero uses greedy selection. This order describes the teaching simulation; provider implementations may differ. This model samples words; real LLMs sample tokens and use much longer contexts.

💬 Discussion question (group activity)

Which settings remove candidates, and which only reshape the probabilities? Find a setting that makes a word with a non-zero base probability impossible to choose. Then generate a few sentences at temperature 0 and at temperature 2: how do they differ, and why?

3

Compare real LLM generations

🔒 Locked. This step opens when you finish a whole sentence in Step 1 (the model has to choose the end marker). Once open, it stays open.

Real chat models work the same way at a much larger scale. Instead of one previous word, their context is everything so far: your prompt and every token they have already written. Instead of a handful of candidates, they score a vocabulary of over 100,000 tokens (words and pieces of words) at every step. The same temperature, top-k and top-p settings are applied to that distribution before each token is drawn.

Here you can't see the distribution, only the text it produces, so you have to infer its shape from repeated outputs. Keep the prompt fixed. Generate at least twice with the same settings, then change one setting and generate twice more. At low temperature, repeated outputs should be more alike; at high temperature, more varied, and eventually less coherent.

Read the outputs as language. Do low-temperature openings reuse the same familiar phrases? Do high-temperature ones choose rarer words or odd combinations? A model that always takes the most likely continuation tends to sound generic, because the most likely sentence is the one most like everything it has read.

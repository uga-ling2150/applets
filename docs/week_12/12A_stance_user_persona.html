Activity 12A: Stance and User Persona

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish evaluative, deontic, epistemic and affective stance in a model's wording.
  • Compare how a model's stance changes when the user persona, the system persona or both are different.
  • Support an interpretation of stance with exact phrases from both answers.

Background

Stance is how a speaker positions themselves toward what they are saying and who they are saying it to. They can judge something as good or bad (evaluative), say what ought to happen (deontic), show how sure they are (epistemic), or express feeling (affective).

In Activity 11A you changed the model's persona through the system prompt. Here you can also change the user, who introduces themselves in their own message before asking the question. The same question goes to two conversations, so if the stance shifts, the personas you changed are the most likely reason. Use exact phrases to support your interpretation.

Instructions

  1. Generate two answers. Keep the default question and personas for your first try, and click Generate both answers.
  2. Highlight the stance. Select a phrase in either answer, choose which of the four stance types it shows, and explain how it relates to the personas. Collect evidence from both answers, and download your work before you leave.

1

Ask the same question in two conversations

A conversation with a chat model has two sides, and each can have a persona. The system persona says who the model is supposed to be, as in Activity 11A. The user persona says who is asking: people mention their job, their age or how they are feeling. That information arrives in the user's own message, and the model can adapt to it, just as you might talk differently to a worried friend and to a colleague.

Here you write the personas yourself. Conversations A and B ask exactly the same question, of the same model, with the same sampling settings. Each conversation needs a system persona; the user persona is optional. The system persona goes in the system prompt, and the user persona goes in the user's message, before the question.

For your first try, keep the defaults and click Generate both answers. The two system personas start out identical, so only the user differs. Neither user asks for a particular tone, so nobody has told the model to change its stance. It has to decide for itself whether who is asking should matter. Then try changing a system persona, to see which side of the conversation moves the stance more.

Stance is built from language. A model has no attitude you can observe directly. Everything that tells you how it is positioning itself, such as what it calls a good idea, what it tells you to do and how sure it sounds, is in the text. That text is the evidence you'll collect in Step 2.
Technical glossary
  • User persona: The identity a user presents in their message. In this activity it is written for the exercise, so use fictional details rather than information about yourself.
  • System persona: The identity the model is told to present, given in the system prompt.
  • User prompt: The message the model receives from the user. Here it is the user persona, if there is one, followed by the question.
  • System prompt: An instruction given to a chat model before the conversation starts. In deployed chatbots it is written by the company, not the user.
  • Stance: How a speaker positions themselves toward what they are saying and who they are saying it to. A single phrase can show more than one type.

2

Highlight the stance

🔒 Locked. This step opens when you generate two answers (or use the practice answers) in Step 1.

Read the two answers side by side, the way a linguist would. The question isn't which answer is better, but how the model positions itself differently in each conversation. Look for small, specific choices: a modal verb such as should or might, a judgement such as a good step, an expression of sympathy. Each answer is a single sample, so generate again before treating a difference as a pattern.

To annotate, drag across a few words in either answer. A small window opens with your phrase filled in. Choose the type of stance it shows, then write a sentence or two linking the wording to the personas: why might the model take this stance here? Your annotations are listed under each answer and highlighted in the text, with one colour, underline style and label for each type. On a touchscreen or keyboard, use the Annotate button under an answer and type the exact words.

Four types of stance to highlight
  • Evaluative: judging something as good or bad, easy or hard, important or not (“a good step”, “tough”, “the better choice”).
  • Deontic: saying what should, must or may happen (“you should”, “you must”, “it’s okay to”, a bare command such as “check the deadline”).
  • Epistemic: showing how certain or how informed the speaker is (“might”, “probably”, “I think”, “clearly”).
  • Affective: expressing or responding to feeling (“I’m sorry”, “don’t worry”, “glad”, an exclamation mark).
Worked example: from phrase to annotation

Phrase: “might” in the Conversation A practice answer.
Stance: Epistemic.
Explanation: “Might” presents the effect on financial aid as a possibility, not a fact. This cautious wording avoids alarming a student who has said they are anxious.

This is a teaching example, not a claim about your own generated answer. Use phrases that actually appear in your answers. If a stance type is missing from one answer, or you see no clear difference between the conversations, that is a finding too: explain it with language evidence.
